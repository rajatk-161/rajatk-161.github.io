I study how molecular interactions and organization govern phase behavior, transport, and structural transformations in supercooled aqueous mixtures and other complex molecular systems.

My work combines molecular simulation, statistical mechanics, and the development and application of atomistic, coarse-grained, and machine-learned models. I have applied these to supercooled water and amorphous ice, water–glycerol mixtures, water and ice at poly(vinyl alcohol) surfaces, and heterogeneous ice nucleation on organic crystals at high pressure, connecting simulations with X-ray scattering and spectroscopy experiments. As a postdoctoral scholar in the Dutagaci Lab at UC Merced from November 2026, I am extending this work to RNA polymerase II, using molecular simulation and machine learning to study how the polymerase and its elongation factors govern transcription.

About

My doctoral research with Valeria Molinero at the University of Utah asked what water does when it is cooled far below its freezing point: whether it separates into two distinct liquids, how that transformation competes with freezing and glass formation, and which of these signatures experiments can actually detect.

The same questions extend to mixtures and surfaces. In water–glycerol mixtures, relevant to cryopreservation, I study how a solute shifts the balance between ice and glass. At poly(vinyl alcohol) surfaces, I study how the polymer structures nearby water to inhibit ice recrystallization. I have also worked on heterogeneous ice nucleation on organic crystals at high pressure. Several of these projects were collaborations with experimental groups.

Before my Ph.D., I simulated ionic liquids at IISER Kolkata, developed ionic-liquid force fields at JNCASR Bengaluru, and benchmarked coarse-graining methods for metal–organic frameworks at the University of Calgary.

Research

Four questions from my doctoral work. The figures are schematics drawn for this page; the papers have the data.

Two liquids and ice in one model

Ice forms fastest right along the line where water’s two liquids meet, yet the freezing temperature carries no trace of the critical point.

With ML-BOP, a machine-learned coarse-grained water model, we found a first-order transition between high- and low-density liquids that ends at a critical point near 170 MPa and 181 K, with a transition line close to that of TIP4P/2005. Because the same model also freezes spontaneously, it let us watch the liquid transformation and crystallization compete directly. Tetrahedral, low-density liquid needs little rearrangement to become ice while staying mobile enough for fast growth. The flip side is that crystallization data alone cannot locate the critical point.

PNAS 2024, co-first author; Valeria Molinero’s National Academy of Sciences Inaugural Article. Covered by Quanta Magazine and a PNAS Profile.

What decompression experiments reveal

The mark of water’s liquid–liquid transition in X-ray scattering is a transient growth of correlations at low wavevector, not a split peak.

Pump–probe X-ray experiments reported two peaks in the structure factor as decompressed water crossed the transition, which would imply two coexisting liquids at a pressure where that should not happen. I ran adiabatic decompression simulations of up to a million molecules, starting below, near, and above the critical temperature. Even on crossing the transition, the main peak stays single because high- and low-density domains remain nanoscopic. The two-peak spectra are better explained by colder liquid lagging behind in a sample with a temperature gradient. The telling signal is the apparent correlation length at low q, which grows and then decays, peaks for paths near the critical point, and stays small when decompression starts above Tc or runs too fast.

J. Chem. Phys. 2026, first author, in the Carlos Vega Festschrift. The paper drew a published comment from the experimental team, and we responded.

How glycerol keeps water from freezing

Glycerol holds off ice by being squeezed between growing low-density water domains, which caps how large ice-ready regions can become.

I built a coarse-grained water–glycerol model, pairing mW water with a flexible six-site glycerol, and parameterized it against mixture densities, enthalpies of mixing, and atomistic first-shell structure. Across 1–50 wt % glycerol, water and glycerol break the Stokes–Einstein relation in different ways, and a dynamical crossover appears consistently in diffusion, viscosity, and hydrogen-bond lifetimes. On cooling, glycerol shifts water’s crossover from high- to low-density local structure to lower temperature without erasing it. The mixture stays homogeneous while glycerol is confined to shrinking gaps, a self-generated soft confinement that limits ice-compatible domains and favors glass over crystal.

Mol. Phys. 2024. First-author manuscript on structural heterogeneity and crystallization to be submitted.

Ice under shear, and water at polymer surfaces

How fast ice is sheared decides which amorphous form of water it becomes.

I contributed to the first molecular-simulation study of medium-density amorphous ice, which established its structural and thermodynamic signatures and identified shear rate as a further dimension of water’s phase diagram. In a multi-institution MURI collaboration on poly(vinyl alcohol) and ice recrystallization inhibition, I analyzed interfacial structure and spatially resolved tetrahedral order to give molecular meaning to EPR and 17O NMR measurements.

PNAS 2024. Covered by @theU and Phys.org. Polymer–water manuscript to be submitted.

Next: the machinery of transcription

In the Dutagaci Lab at UC Merced, I will use molecular dynamics, enhanced sampling, and machine learning to study how RNA polymerase II and its elongation factors control transcription, as part of the lab’s NIH-funded project on elongation complex dynamics. The question carries over from water: which fluctuations matter, and which states compete.
