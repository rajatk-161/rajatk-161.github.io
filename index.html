Rajat Kumar

I study how molecular interactions and organization govern phase behavior, transport, and structural transformations in supercooled aqueous mixtures and other complex molecular systems.

My work combines molecular simulation, statistical mechanics, and the development and application of atomistic, coarse-grained, and machine-learned models. I have applied these to the liquid–liquid transition and ice crystallization of supercooled water, amorphous ices, water–glycerol mixtures, water structuring at poly(vinyl alcohol) surfaces, and heterogeneous ice nucleation on organic crystals at high pressure, and to the interpretation of X-ray scattering and spectroscopic measurements.

As a postdoctoral scholar in the Dutagaci Lab at UC Merced from November 2026, I am extending this work to RNA polymerase II, using molecular simulation and machine learning to study how the polymerase and its elongation factors govern transcription.

About

My doctoral research with Prof. Valeria Molinero at the University of Utah addressed supercooled water: whether it undergoes a liquid–liquid transition between high- and low-density liquids, how that transition competes with ice crystallization and vitrification, and which of its structural signatures are accessible to experiment.

I extended these questions to aqueous mixtures and interfaces. In water–glycerol mixtures, which are widely used as cryoprotectants, I studied how glycerol alters water’s structural transformation and dynamics and the competition between crystallization and vitrification. In a multi-institution collaboration, I studied water structuring at poly(vinyl alcohol) surfaces and its relation to ice recrystallization inhibition. I have also worked on heterogeneous ice nucleation on organic crystals at high pressure.

Before my Ph.D., I studied the structure and dynamics of imidazolium-based ionic liquids in solvents of varying polarity using molecular simulations at IISER Kolkata, evaluated force fields for imidazolium-based ionic liquids at JNCASR Bengaluru, and parameterized coarse-grained force fields for metal–organic frameworks at the University of Calgary.

Research

Liquid–liquid transition and ice crystallization in one model

Ice crystallization is fastest at the liquid–liquid transition and its supercritical continuation, yet the crystallization temperatures show no signature of the liquid–liquid critical point.

We showed that ML-BOP, a machine-learned coarse-grained water model, has a liquid–liquid transition that ends at a critical point at pc = 170 ± 10 MPa and Tc = 181 ± 3 K, with a transition line almost identical to that of the all-atom TIP4P/2005 model. Because ML-BOP is more than 100 times more computationally efficient than all-atom models and crystallizes on accessible timescales, the liquid–liquid transition and ice formation can be studied in the same simulations. Cooling simulations show that the temperature of maximum crystallization rate coincides with the liquid–liquid transition line and its supercritical continuation of maximum heat capacity, supporting a mechanistic relationship between the formation of low-density liquid and ice. Decompression simulations of the high-density liquid reproduce the competition between low-density liquid and ice formation observed in ultrafast experiments, with ice crystallizing before the high- and low-density domains coarsen.

PNAS 2024, co-first author; National Academy of Sciences Inaugural Article of Valeria Molinero. Covered by Quanta Magazine and a PNAS Profile.

What decompression experiments reveal

The structural signature of water’s liquid–liquid transition in decompression experiments is a transient growth and decay of the apparent correlation length at low q, not a split peak in the structure factor.

Pump–probe X-ray experiments interpreted two peaks in the structure factor S(q) during decompression of high-density liquid as evidence of its coexistence with low-density liquid, which is implausible at ambient pressure, below the estimated critical pressure. Using adiabatic decompression simulations of ML-BOP with up to 106 molecules, starting below, at, and above Tc, we found that S(q) retains a single peak even when water decompresses along the liquid–liquid transition, because the high- and low-density domains remain nanoscopic. We attribute the two experimental peaks to a single evolving liquid peak superimposed on a slower-responding, colder high-density liquid that arises from the temperature gradient across the sample. The apparent correlation length ξ at low q grows and decays only when decompression proceeds along the transition line, reaching its maximum near the critical point; it remains low when decompression starts at or above Tc or proceeds too rapidly.

J. Chem. Phys. 2026, first author; Carlos Vega Festschrift. The experimental authors published a comment, to which we published a response.

Glycerol, water structure, and cryoprotection

Glycerol suppresses ice crystallization by softly confining low-density water domains below the size needed for ice nucleation, rather than by solute exclusion.

Using all-atom and coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations, we characterized diffusion, viscosity, hydrogen-bond dynamics, the density anomaly, and the structure of water with 1–50 wt % glycerol at low temperatures. Water and glycerol show distinct violations of the Stokes–Einstein relation, and both models reveal a crossover in the dynamics of the 1 and 10 wt % mixtures at the lowest temperatures, which we attribute to the opposing concentration dependence of tetrahedrally coordinated water and of glycerol dynamics and clustering. The coarse-grained model combines mW water with a flexible six-site glycerol, parameterized against mixture densities, enthalpies of mixing, and atomistic first-shell structure. In a first-author manuscript, I use it to show that for 0–30 wt % glycerol at 1 bar, glycerol shifts water’s structural crossover to lower temperature without suppressing its two-state response. The mixtures remain single-phase: the glass contains interpenetrating low-density-rich and high-density-rich domains, and glycerol acts as a soft confining agent rather than coarsening toward nanophase segregation.

Mol. Phys. 2024. First-author manuscript on soft confinement and crystallization in water–glycerol mixtures to be submitted.
